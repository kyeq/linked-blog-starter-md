# 1.10 Programming vs Software Engineering
> [!info] From the CS-128 notes on Canvas — [open the original page](https://canvas.swansea.ac.uk/courses/68227/pages/1-dot-10-programming-vs-software-engineering)

Some of you may be doing a degree in Software Engineering - and might be wondering what the difference is (if any) between programming and software engineering. A lot of you will work in software engineering and may well think they are the same - they are not.

- Programming is about the details of writing (and testing) code.
- Software Engineering is about  *managing* programming (and testing and the other parts of  *software development)*

### 1.10.1 Some History

When computers were first developed the problem (everyone thought) was building the hardware - the software was considered to be the easy part. But as the 1960s progressed, more and more big software projects failed. There is a famous book -  *The Mythical Man-Month* by Fred Brooks (it's in the library and it's a quick and easy read) that recounts the huge problems that IBM (then by far the biggest computer company in the world) had when developing the operating system for its new computer system - the 360 series. The key message was: just adding more resources (people, money) to big software projects did not make them go quicker - in fact the opposite, because  *how* to  *organise, manage* and  *control* these big software projects was not understood.

In 1968 a famous conference invented the terms "the software crisis" and  *software engineering -* and it became clear that there needed to be something other than just programming if software projects were to succeed. There then followed many decades of development of ways of doing that - and progressive improvements in the reliability and success of software projects. Or at least, it was  *possible* for projects to be well-engineered, managed and successful - that is not to say that they all are, and there are still big software project failures.

### 1.10.2 Why is Software Engineering Hard?

Three reasons mainly.

- **Software Systems are Complex.**  Actually, the most conceptually complex things that humans build - at least the big ones. So they are arguably intrinsically more "difficult" than other kinds of engineering (in one sense - and other engineers might not agree of course).
- **We are Still New to This.** We have been engineering buildings, bridges etc. for thousands of years, and have progressively learned. Software is much newer than that. (Having said that, other fields of engineering - electrical, electronic, chemical, materials - are pretty new too).
- **"Software Is Easy to Change".**  It's only code right? You can easily change it, it's not like, say, a bridge? Thinking like this means that projects often had unclear goals to start with, and those goals often changed while the software was being developed. There was always this assumption (mostly by the  *customers* - not by the  *engineers* ) that you could always change what you wanted later, and it would be easy. Partly this was also because we were (and still are a bit) not very good at clearly deciding what it should do (as in do  *exactly* ) in the first place. This doesn't tend to happen with other engineering projects - once you've designed and started building a bridge then you usually don't change it (unless you find a [problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millennium_Bridge,_London)) - you wouldn't expect while half-way through someone would ask, say, that it carry trains as well as having a road.
- **Software is  *Discontinuous* .**  By this I mean it's not so easy to predict what it will do which is related to its complexity but also because it's digital or  *discrete* . If you are building bridges not only do you have hundreds of years of experience to call on, but also their behaviour is predictable and can be  *extrapolated.* If you drive vehicles over it, it bends; drive more vehicles and bends more; eventually, it will break. This can be predicted and modelled. Software is like a bridge in which you can drive as many vehicles over it as you want and nothing happens, but if there is one green bus, the whole thing collapses. (This is not entirely true - for example behaviour under  *load* can be modelled usually: but there can still be massive discontinuous changes caused by the logic.)

So this means it is hard, and it took a long time to get to a point in which we are reasonably good at doing it.

### 1.10.3 Software Engineering Terms

Generally we talk about software projects in these terms.

- **Requirements.**  What is it supposed to  *achieve* ? For example, a requirement might be "humans need a way to travel"; or "we need a bridge over this river".
- **Specification.** How are we supposed to  *satisfy* the requirements. For example, for the travel requirement, the specification could be a car, a bus, a bike, a plane, walking... etc. Usually when we are talking about software engineering, the proposed solution doesn't exist yet (the software isn't written). The possible solutions have pros and cons - e.g. walking is cheap and low-carbon but slow; planes are quick but expensive and high-carbon - and there may need to be decisions made about which option is chosen. For the bridge example, it would be the type and design of bridge. Of course in reality, especially with software, it can often be difficult to work out if what you are proposing to do (specification) does actually meet the requirements.
- **Implementation.** The actual solution itself - which should  *meet* the specification and hence  *satisfy* the requirements.
- **Testing/Verification.**  Covers many things and we will get to this later. But broadly: do the parts of the system work (individually and together); and does it ultimately do what we want it to do correctly? There is also the question of how do we  *decide* if something meets it's specification (and so works properly).
- **Maintenance.**  Not the normal meaning of "it's broken/worn out, we need to fix it" - but changes, developments, enhancements and - possibly - modifications to keep up with changes to e.g. operating systems through the life of the project.

This sequence of stages (sometimes with slightly different names and/or slightly different stages) is called the  *Software Lifecycle.*

Very amusing - but what is left out is that in practice the user (or  *stakeholder* as we'd more likely say today) may have wanted the tyre swing,  *but they may not have known that themselves;* and  *they may have changed their mind about what they wanted as the project was developed;* and  *even if they did know what they wanted, they may not have had a way of clearing explaining what they wanted.* Also:  *how do you explain to the customer what you are planning to build?* Plus there are possible misunderstandings between those writing the requirements, those writing the specification, those writing the code, and the end user/customer/stakeholder. There is also the big problem of  *managing* the project - making sure it stays on track; doesn't overrun in time (or cost); that  *risks* (things you anticipate could go wrong) are identified and  *managed.* And all this at a time when nobody had worked out any  *language* or  *notation* to express what was wanted. So all this is actually difficult, but important to solve.

### 1.10.5 Software Engineering Models

Despite the problems, people had a go at coming up with ways of engineering software projects - called  *software engineering models* or  *software development models* . There are quite a few of these - and here are three (really the last one covers several variations and we'll get back to some of those).

#### Waterfall Model

The obvious thing to do - the stages seem to have a natural order, so do them in that order and don't move on to the next one until the previous one is done:

*(image on Canvas: Waterfall Model)*

This is so obvious it's been in use for a very long time - way before the idea of software engineering was invented - but in a fairly informal way. The first person to  *call* it the Waterfall Model did that in 1976, but the first person to really talk about in detail did that in 1970 - and it's often overlooked that he used it as example of how  *not* to do it... (sort of, not exactly). The problem with this is the idea that any of the early stages can be 'finished' because people change them minds about what they want; or it turns out that there was some misunderstanding; or a way of implementing something that people assumed (rightly or wrongly) would work didn't. In practice this only really stands a chance of working if you are  *very* sure you are not going to change things; you are not trying anything you haven't tried before (so no chance of things not working); and you are happier for the product to be a bit late/over budget than not do exactly what you wanted in the first place. This is not common, so it's rarely a good option these days. (Some people propose using this in their final year projects -  ***it is absolutely the worst possible choice for that***  - you don't know exactly what you are doing; you don't know everything will work; you don't have a fixed end point but you do have an absolutely fixed deadline. Please remember this when the time comes.)

#### Spiral Model

This modifies the Waterfall Model by accepting that each stage won't be definitely finalised, and allows you to revisit them:

*(image on Canvas: Spiral Model)*

- This diagram has a number of parts missing from Waterfall (and our list) - some of these are just the others broken down into more detail, but a couple are new -  *risk analysis* and  *prototyping.* But the main things here are: You circle back through the early stages of the development process, refining the requirement.
- You build a series of  *prototypes*  (i.e. simplified, quickly-built versions of the applications).
- You do design verification and validation which would probably in most cases be asking the ultimate end user: "Is this what you meant? Having seen this, are there things you want to change?"
- Only when everyone is happy, do you go ahead and start building the "real" version.

#### Agile

Really a term that covers a lot of different approaches - but they all have things in common. They drop the rigid, formal definition of what is required, and get the stakeholders and the developers to  *collaborate* on the development process. So rather than starting with a formal requirements that tries to cover the whole project, you start building by doing  *part* of what the customer asks for - probably the most clear and obvious part that they are definitely going to need and which is not likely to change (much). This potentially gets them  *something*  working quite quickly, even if it doesn't do everything they want - and it also gives them a bit more insight into  *what else* they need (as opposed to what they thought they wanted) because now they have something, although basic, working. Then you go on to do the next thing which is now probably clearer given you have the first part. A key difference between this and spiral is that you are not building prototypes - you are working on the real thing from quite an early stage. This is a massive simplification for what is really a complex process, but agile development (in many different forms) is now a very common model of software engineering. (And some version of it is usually the best option for final year projects.)
